iv. Abstract (English)

Ally is a research prototype that uses a laptop and a single webcam to watch over one older adult living alone in one living-area room. It runs pose estimation locally on the laptop GPU, extracts kinematic features over a 1–2 second window and classifies falls with a temporal model, while also detecting prolonged inactivity and reading a mood cue from facial expression. What separates Ally from every shipped product surveyed in Chapter 2 is the middle step: when an event fires, Ally talks to the person first — it asks aloud whether they are all right, listens for a reply, and notifies a guardian over Telegram only after verification fails or goes unanswered. The escalation decision is owned by an on-device finite-state machine, never by the language model, which may only label a reply fine, help or unclear. Raw video is never written to disk and never leaves the device; only conversation text, a JSON state summary and one keyframe per event cross the privacy boundary. This report presents the background study, system requirements, architectural design, the intelligent-computing methods and their construction, the implementation, and the testing and evaluation plan.

Note: this abstract describes the system as designed. Evaluation results are inserted after Sprint S8 (5–18 April 2027); the abstract is rewritten at code freeze to lead with the measured fall recall, false-alarm rate and usability score.

1Introduction

1.1 Background

A fall is not dangerous only because of the impact. It is dangerous because of the hours that can follow it. The World Health Organization records that an estimated 684 000 individuals die from falls globally each year, that falls are the second leading cause of unintentional injury deaths worldwide, that adults older than 60 years suffer the greatest number of fatal falls, and that 37.3 million falls severe enough to require medical attention occur each year [1]. Malaysia is ageing into exactly this risk. The share of the population aged 60 and over stood at 7.9 per cent in 2010 and is projected to reach 15.3 per cent by 2030 [2]. A statistic this report deliberately does not make. No verified Malaysian statistic on older adults living alone was found during research for the project plan. The obvious sentence — “X per cent of Malaysian seniors live alone” — is therefore absent from this report and will stay absent unless a DOSM or peer-reviewed source is found. The premise of the project does not depend on it.

What the sourced facts support is narrower and sufficient: falls among older adults are common, frequently serious, and the population at risk in Malaysia is growing. The gap this project addresses is the one between the fall and the help — the interval in which nobody knows anything has happened.

1.2 Problem statement

The existing responses to that interval each break in a predictable way.

  • A wearable pendant does nothing if it is not worn. Compliance is the failure mode, and it bites exactly when the device is least convenient — at night, in the shower, after a shower.
  • A camera that only records does not summon anyone. Footage is evidence after the fact, not a response.
  • A camera that only alerts creates a different problem: false alarms. A system that messages a working adult several times a week for a person lying on a sofa gets switched off, and a switched-off system detects nothing.
  • None of them keeps the person company. Isolation is the condition that makes living alone risky in the first place, and a monitoring device that treats the person as a subject rather than someone to talk to does not touch it.

The problem, stated as one sentence: an older adult living alone can fall, or be unwell, and remain unnoticed for hours, while the technologies that could notice either depend on being worn, or report to a third party without ever engaging the person themselves.

1.3 Motivation

The middle step is missing. Between “something happened” and “call the family” there is a question a human would ask first: are you all right? Asking it does three things at once. It reaches the person immediately, with a voice, when they may be frightened and alone. It gathers the context that decides whether this is an emergency — a reply of “I'm fine, I dropped something” ends the incident. And it filters the alerts, so that the notifications a guardian does receive are worth their attention. A system that asks first can afford to be sensitive, because the conversation absorbs the errors.

1.5 Proposed solution

Ally is a laptop and a single fixed webcam in one living-area room. Its pipeline is deliberately shaped so that the expensive, cloud-based reasoning happens rarely and the cheap, local perception happens continuously.

  1. See. OpenCV captures 640×480 frames. A pose model on the laptop GPU turns each frame into keypoints. Frames live in a 10-second RAM ring buffer and are never written to disk.
  2. Judge. Kinematic features over a 1–2 second window feed a temporal classifier for falls; keypoint motion energy feeds an inactivity timer; a small CNN on detected faces feeds a smoothed mood estimate.
  3. Decide. A pure finite-state machine — no camera, no network, no clock of its own — moves from NORMAL through SUSPECTED_* and VERIFY to CONFIRMED, STOOD_DOWN or FALSE_ALARM.
  4. Talk. On entering VERIFY, Ally speaks immediately from canned audio, then converses through speech-to-text, a cloud language model and text-to-speech. In parallel it sends one keyframe to a vision model and gets a structured second opinion.
  5. Escalate, only then. If the person says they are fine and the vision model does not contradict it, Ally stands down. If the reply is help or unclear, or there is no reply after two prompts, the state machine confirms and creates the notification. Telegram carries the event type, the time, the keyframe and the transcript.
The architectural commitment that defines this project (ADR-001). The language model has no tool that sends a Telegram message. In VERIFY its only output is a label — fine, help or unclear. It may request escalation or stand-down; the event engine decides. Speech-to-text output is treated as untrusted input, so a voice from a television saying “don't call anyone” cannot suppress an alert. The safety-critical path contains no model output that has not passed through a state machine with a unit test on every transition.

1.6 Benefits and significance

  • For the monitored person: help arrives even when a phone is out of reach, nothing must be worn, and the system speaks to them rather than about them.
  • For the guardian: an alert that has already been checked, arriving with a picture and a transcript, so that urgency is judged in seconds rather than by calling to find out.
  • For privacy: the family's living room is not streamed to a server. Raw video stays in RAM on a machine in the house.
  • Academically: the project joins continuous local perception to proactive dialogue and measures what the join is worth — specifically, how many false alarms the verification step removes, and how many true events it costs. That ablation is the report's central empirical claim.
  • SDG alignment: SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being) through injury response and reduced isolation; SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities) through low-cost hardware rather than a subscription care service.

2.2 Falls, ageing and the response gap

The quantitative basis is given in §1.1 and is not repeated. Three qualitative points shape the design.

  • Time on the floor is the variable that matters. The severity of a fall's outcome depends heavily on how long the person lies there before help arrives. This is the variable a detection system can actually change, and it is why detection latency and notification latency are separate budgeted numbers in NFR-1.
  • Compliance defeats wearables. Any device requiring the person to wear or carry it inherits their memory and their willingness. An ambient sensor inherits neither.
  • Alert fatigue defeats alerting. A guardian who receives a false alert daily stops reading them. This is why the false-alarm budget in this project is deliberately split into two tiers (ADR-003): what the perception layer may suspect, and what may actually reach a human.

2.5 System architecture layers

The reference report organises its architecture into client, AI, backend and storage layers. Ally's layering is different because its defining constraint is a privacy boundary rather than a client-server split.

  1. Capture layer — webcam, OpenCV, fixed at 640×480, feeding a bounded drop-oldest queue and a 10-second RAM ring buffer.
  2. Local perception layer — pose estimation, fall features and classifier, presence and inactivity, face detection and FER. Runs on the laptop GPU. Emits only keypoints and scalars, never pixels.
  3. Fusion layer — the pure finite-state machine, cooldowns and person state. No I/O of any kind: no camera, no network, and time is passed in rather than read. This is what makes it unit-testable transition by transition.
  4. Interaction layer — speech I/O locally; dialogue and keyframe verification through a cloud model behind a single client interface.
  5. Persistence and delivery layer — SQLite for events, transcripts and mood samples; a seven-day keyframe retention job; a LAN-only dashboard; the Telegram bot with a single-chat-ID allowlist.

2.6 Summary

The problem is sized by primary sources. The gap is specific and is not closed by any surveyed product. The methods chosen are the ones that fit a 4 GB GPU, a privacy rule and a solo schedule, and each was chosen against a named alternative that Chapter 5 must justify with measurements rather than assertion.

3.4 Methodology: Scrum adapted to one developer

The project runs two-week sprints with a single ordered backlog and four ceremonies. The adaptation is that the developer holds the Product Owner role while an orchestrating agent session acts as Scrum Master and a coach agent enforces process before any work is delegated.

3.4.1 Definition of Ready

An item may enter a sprint only when all five hold: (1) it is one small vertical slice doable in one session; (2) its acceptance criteria are observable — a test, a table, a file, or a number with its unit; (3) every prerequisite is done or explicitly named as the blocker; (4) the owner is named; (5) it serves a user story, a requirement or a course deliverable. An item that fails any of these stays in the backlog with the blocker written down.

3.4.2 Definition of Done

Tests and lint green; if perception or fusion was touched, every recorded replay session still yields its expected events; if the agent was touched, escalation-decision accuracy on the scenario suite is unchanged or better and the injection cases still pass; any new decision or number is recorded with its rationale; five lines are written in the engineering journal; and what was verified — and what was not run — is reported explicitly.

3.4.3 Ceremonies

  • Sprint planning on the sprint's first Monday: set the Sprint Goal, pull ready items, human confirms.
  • Stand-up at the start of every working session: done since last, doing now, blocked — then a process check.
  • Sprint review on the last Friday: each exit criterion demonstrated or not, with evidence.
  • Retrospective immediately after: what to keep, what to change, one process action for the next sprint.

Only one item may be in progress at a time. Work not on the backlog is not started; an agent that discovers work proposes a backlog item rather than doing it.

4.2.1 Runtime model

  • main.py starts workers connected by bounded, drop-oldest queues: capture → perception → fusion → agent. Perception must hold ≥ 15 FPS, so a slow consumer drops frames rather than applying back-pressure to capture. A monitoring system that stutters under load is worse than one that skips a frame.
  • Frames live in a RAM ring buffer holding the last 10 seconds, so a keyframe from just before the event can be chosen. They are never written to disk. At 640×480 this is roughly 140 MB; at 1080p it would be roughly 930 MB, which is why the capture resolution is fixed rather than configurable.
  • GPU placement: pose and FER on the RTX 2050; faster-whisper (int8) and Piper on CPU.
  • --replay <session> replaces the webcam with a recording that contains keypoints, audio and events — never raw frames. Everything from perception onward runs the same code path (ADR-004).

5.2 Construction — fall detector

  1. Data. Download UR Fall and Le2i; extract RGB frames; run the pose model on every frame; store per-frame keypoints (x, y, confidence) as NumPy arrays. A window is labelled “fall” if it overlaps the annotated fall interval. DONE — see §6.2.4
  2. Features. Per frame: torso angle to vertical (shoulder centre to hip centre), bounding-box aspect ratio, normalised hip height, vertical velocity and acceleration of the hip centre by finite differences, and mean keypoint confidence. Per window (1.5 s, about 22 frames at 15 FPS): the raw sequence for the GRU, and minimum / maximum / mean / standard deviation for the tree baseline. DONE — implemented and unit-tested
  3. Baselines. (a) Rule: torso angle above 60° and vertical velocity above v_thr, followed by low hip height sustained for 2 s. (b) Gradient-boosted trees on the window statistics. (a) implemented, not yet run on the dataset; (b) not started
  4. Temporal model. A one-layer GRU (hidden size 64) with a sigmoid output and class-weighted binary cross-entropy. Leave-one-subject-out: train on all subjects but one, test on the held out subject, rotate; early stopping on a validation fold's F1; report the mean and confidence interval across folds.
  5. Threshold. Choose τfall on the validation folds for recall ≥ 0.90; report specificity and the suspected-event false-alarm rate per hour on self-recorded footage, then the guardian-level rate per day after the verifier.
  6. Ablation. Rule versus trees versus GRU; and with versus without VLM second-stage verification. How many false alarms the verifier removes, and how many true events it costs, is this project's central improvement claim.
  7. Real time. Export to ONNX; run in a worker thread fed by a bounded drop-oldest queue; profile FPS and VRAM on the RTX 2050; record a session and confirm that replay yields identical events.
Honest status of step 3. The rule detector exists and is unit-tested against synthetic sequences, but it has never been run against the extracted UR Fall keypoints. Two thresholds (v_thr and hip_height_max) are deliberately left required-not-defaulted in the code so that no value can slip in unnoticed, and the developer — who owns every number — has not yet set them. The subject map needed for leave-one-subject-out is also unfilled, because UR Fall does not publish one and it must be built by hand rather than guessed.

5.3 Construction — emotion cue

  1. Face detection and alignment; crop to 48×48 greyscale (the FER-2013 format) or 112×112 RGB.
  2. Fine-tune a small backbone on the FER-2013 training split; evaluate on its test split; produce a confusion matrix. Fallback if Sprint S5 slips: pretrained weights without fine-tuning for the March demo.
  3. Map the 7 classes to a valence in [−1, 1]; smooth with an exponential moving average; expose the mood to the event engine.
  4. State the limitation in the report: expression is not emotion. The cue triggers a gentle conversation and nothing else.

5.4 Construction — dialogue agent

  1. A persona system prompt: calm, brief, never gives medical advice, offers to contact the guardian when in doubt. Kept byte-stable so it can be prompt-cached.
  2. Tools as JSON schemas with strict validation; each is a Python function whose side effect is a request to the event engine, never a direct notification.
  3. A reply classifier returning fine, help or unclear via structured outputs; the state machine consumes the label.
  4. Wire the loop: state JSON and transcript → model → text and/or tool call → execute → TTS, streamed so that speech begins on the first sentence. Choose the dialogue-route effort setting by measuring first-audio latency against NFR-1, and record the chosen model ID and effort in configuration.
  5. A 30-scenario harness: fall and responsive, fall and silent, false alarm, sad mood, request for help, off-topic, and “don't call anyone” plus television-audio injection. The harness auto-checks the state machine's decision; two raters score the wording; each scenario runs five times.
  6. An offline fallback: a scripted state machine with pre-recorded prompts and keyword spotting; unanswered leads to a local audible alarm and a queued retry.

5.5 Cloud model budget

A provider-independent estimate: 20 conversations per day at roughly 2 000 input and 300 output tokens, plus 50 verifications per day at roughly 1 500 input (including the image) and 100 output tokens, gives approximately 3.5 M input and 0.33 M output tokens per month. At the Opus-tier reference pricing recorded in the plan (roughly US$5 and US$25 per million input and output tokens [18]) that is around US$25 per month; running verification on a Sonnet-tier model roughly halves it, and prompt-caching the stable persona reduces it further. The NFR-6 budget of RM 100 per month is comfortable, and a hard spend limit is set in the provider console. SECONDARY — re-check live pricing and pin the exact model ID at SRD time

8Ethics, Privacy, Safety and Security

8.1 Safety claims and limitations

Ally is a research prototype. It is not a medical device and not an emergency-response device. This statement appears in this report, in the repository README and in the spoken explanation Ally gives on first run. Ally supplements human contact and emergency services; it does not replace them.
  • Offline: Ally still detects, checks in and sounds a local alarm, but no remote person is notified until connectivity returns.
  • Emotion: expression is not emotion. FER datasets are not representative of all populations. The cue only triggers a gentle conversation and never raises an alert on its own.
  • Coverage: one fixed camera in one room has occlusion blind spots, and performance across lighting conditions is a reported result rather than an assumed one.

8.2 Consent and participants

  • Every person who appears on camera during development or testing signs a consent form stating the purpose, what is stored, for how long, and the right to withdraw. Forms are kept with the project records.
  • No children are recorded or used as test subjects at any stage. The child use case is future work only.
  • Whether School ethics clearance is required for the Sprint S9 user testing must be confirmed with the supervisor well before that sprint — it is logged as risk R6 precisely because clearance takes time.
  • Staged falls: adults only; thick mattress or crash mat; no falls from standing height for anyone with a health condition; a second person present throughout.

8.3 Data minimisation and the cloud boundary

  • No continuous video recording. Frames are processed in RAM. Only one keyframe per event is persisted, for seven days, deletable from the dashboard, with a face-blur option on by default for guardian messages.
  • Session recording stores keypoints, audio and events — never raw frames.
  • Living areas only; bathrooms and bedrooms are excluded by design.
  • Only text and one keyframe per event reach the API provider; the provider's data-retention terms are to be documented in the final report. ALLY_TEXT_ONLY_VERIFY=true disables image upload entirely.

8.4 Transparency to the monitored person

A person being watched should be able to tell when, and be able to stop it. Ally therefore requires a visible indicator when it is listening or speaking, a spoken explanation on first run, and a physical pause-monitoring control that the state machine honours. These are requirements on the interface design in §4.7, not optional polish.
